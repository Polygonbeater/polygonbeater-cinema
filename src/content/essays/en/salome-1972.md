> *“I didn't want to retell Wilde, but to question the impossibility of martyrdom in a modern world, which is no longer barbaric, but exclusively stupid.”*

Carmelo Bene’s *Salomè* (1972) stands as a radical, iconoclastic zenith of the Italian "Anti-Cinema" movement. Eschewing conventional narrative structures, Bene delivers a destructive, highly stylized adaptation of Oscar Wilde’s play that violently subverts cinematic norms and audience expectations.

## The Anti-Plot and Baroque Aesthetics

Bene rejects psychological realism and traditional storytelling in favor of a subversive visual collage:

* **Subversion of Expectations:** Key dramatic moments are deliberately withheld; the famous Dance of the Seven Veils is skipped entirely as the veils are simply folded away, and John the Baptist's execution is replaced by the grotesque slicing of a watermelon.
* **Baroque and Pop Art Synthesis:** The film revels in fluorescent colors, glitter, optical solarization, and mirror reflections, fusing Lecce Baroque opulence with vulgar Pop Art kitsch.
* **Linguistic Provocation:** High-flown rhetorical dialogue clashes directly with jarring colloquialisms, such as John the Baptist speaking in a regional Puglian dialect.

> *„Salomè remains an uncompromising experimental masterwork, blending the high literature of Oscar Wilde with visual perversion and an unapologetic aesthetic of decay.“*
