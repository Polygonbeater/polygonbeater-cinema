When Polish director Michał Waszyński brought S. Ansky's renowned play *The Dybbuk* (*Der Dibuk*) to the silver screen in 1937, he did not merely create a film. He constructed a visual monument to Eastern European Jewish culture, mysticism, and Hasidic folklore. Shot entirely in Yiddish, the picture is today regarded as one of the most vital ethnographic and artistic documents of the shtetl—a world that would, only a few years later, vanish forever in the flames of the Second World War.

This study examines the film as a fascinating relic where dark Kabbalah intertwines with daily rituals, and where death does not signify the end of love, but merely a transition into a different form of existence.

### 1. Premise and Folkloric Structure: Kabbalah and the Power of an Oath
At the heart of Hasidic tradition lies an immense reverence for a given word. When two friends, Sender and Nisan, pledge before God that their unborn children (should they be a boy and a girl) will one day marry, they forge a bond that transcends the physical realm. However, Sender eventually breaks this sacred vow, promising his daughter Lea (Lili Liliana) to a wealthy merchant instead.

Here, ancient Jewish mysticism enters the narrative. Nisan's son, a poor yeshiva student named Chanon (Leon Liebgold), turns away from traditional Torah study in his despair and delves into the forbidden texts of the Kabbalah. He attempts to manipulate reality using dark magical incantations. The price for this transgression is fatal—Chanon is struck dead by lightning. Yet his soul, fueled by unquenchable love and a sense of profound injustice, refuses to cross over to the other side. It becomes a dybbuk—a wandering entity seeking a vessel.¹

### 2. The Dance of the Beggars and the Rituals of the Shtetl
The film serves as a masterful showcase of the customs and superstitions of the era. The most striking folkloric element of the movie is the famous and visually spellbinding "Dance of the Beggars" (*Totentanz*) at Lea's wedding. According to tradition, the poorest members of the community had the right to dance with the bride to ensure good fortune. Waszyński shoots this scene at an expressionist, almost hallucinatory pace, where the beggars morph into grotesque, ominous figures resembling messengers from the afterlife. It is precisely within this macabre whirlwind that Chanon's dybbuk enters Lea's body. The bride suddenly speaks with the male voice of her dead lover.²

### 3. Epilogue: Exorcism and the Triumph of Spiritual Love
The film's climax brings us to the figure of the Tzadik (a righteous, miracle-working rabbi), who represents the highest spiritual authority of the community. The subsequent exorcism ritual is not just a dramatic cinematic scene, but a meticulous reconstruction of ancient Kabbalistic practices—from the blowing of the ram's horn (shofar) to the lighting of black candles and the recitation of the anathema.

Although the dybbuk is ultimately expelled from the girl's body, this folkloric tale does not conclude with a Hollywood happy ending. Lea realizes that her earthly life is empty without Chanon. The film culminates in a deeply romantic and mystical union—Lea voluntarily departs the world of the living so her soul can merge with her predestined mate in the darkness of eternity. *The Dybbuk* is therefore not primarily a horror film, but the darkest and most beautiful fairy tale about a love that literally conquered death.³
