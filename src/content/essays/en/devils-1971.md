This study deconstructs Ken Russell's extremely controversial and historically censored historical drama *The Devils* (1971, running time 111 minutes) as a radical critique of the political weaponization of faith. Based on historical events in 17th-century Loudun, France, the film analyzes the destruction of a nonconformist priest, Urbain Grandier, who falls victim to fabricated witchcraft accusations orchestrated by sexually frustrated nuns and Cardinal Richelieu. 

Methodologically, the text focuses on Derek Jarman's anachronistic set design (the city as a clinical hospital/abattoir), the psychosomatic nature of supposed possession, and the deconstruction of the Passion of Christ. The film presents a twisted Easter allegory where the true monster is not the devil, but the institutions of Church and State themselves.

### I. The White City and the Architecture of Repression

The visual concept of the film completely rejects traditional historical realism. The design of the city of Loudun was created by the then-emerging Derek Jarman, who built monumental, blindingly white tiled sets. The city does not resemble the 17th century, but rather a modern psychiatric hospital, crematorium, or public bathhouse. 

This sterile horizontal symbolizes the cold, bureaucratic power of the State and the Church. Crosses, altars, and relics lose their sacred aura here, becoming mere props in a political theater. In this clinical space, there is no room for a real God, only for the calculating politics of Cardinal Richelieu, who needs to break the city's independence.

### II. The Stigmata of Hysteria and the Suppressed Vertical of the Body

The key figure of psychological terror is Sister Jeanne (Vanessa Redgrave), the hunchbacked prioress of the Ursuline convent. Her supposed "demonic possession" is not of supernatural origin; it is a pure, explosive psychosomatic reaction to sexual frustration and physical deformity. 

Her obsession with Grandier (Oliver Reed) transforms into a mass hysteria enveloping the entire convent. Russell brilliantly illustrates how the suppressed bodily vertical (human sexuality) erupts to the surface in a pathological, religiously sanctioned form. The famous (and often excised) scenes of the nuns' orgies before the altar are not gratuitous blasphemy, but proof that asceticism and the denial of human nature breed madness.

### III. The False Christ and the Deconstruction of the Passion

The Easter motif of crucifixion and martyrdom is taken to absolute physical brutality in *The Devils*. Grandier, though initially an arrogant and lustful sinner, becomes a true Christ figure in the face of his political trial. 

His trial and subsequent torture (the crushing of his legs, the shaving of his hair and beard) function as a literal Stations of the Cross. The Church inquisitors do not demand the truth from him, but the confession of a lie—they demand he admit to being a servant of Satan to justify the destruction of the city. By refusing to sell his soul to a state lie and enduring unspeakable agony, Grandier achieves true martyrdom, in stark contrast to the theatrical "suffering" of the hysterical nuns.

### IV. Conclusion: Purification by Fire and the Fall of the Walls

The final act of the film denies any hope of divine intervention or justice. Grandier is burned at the stake before the eyes of the citizens and the nuns, while the city walls are simultaneously demolished by royal cannons. There is no resurrection here. 

Russell's film ends with a vision of the absolute triumph of power over the individual. The fire does not cleanse sins; it burns away the last remnants of truth and humanity. In a chilling epilogue, we see a devastated Sister Jeanne clutching Grandier's charred bone in the ruins as a false relic of her own guilt. *The Devils* is cinema's darkest Easter—a crucifixion orchestrated by those who proclaim themselves to be holy.
