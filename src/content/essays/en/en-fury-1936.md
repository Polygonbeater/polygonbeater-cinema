*Fury* (1936), produced by Joseph L. Mankiewicz at Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, marks Fritz Lang’s stunning American debut. Drawing inspiration from real-world atrocities—specifically the 1933 San Jose lynching—Lang crafts a controlled study of how ordinary communities transform into destructive mobs.

### I. The Mob as a System and the Manufacture of Certainty

Lang refuses to frame the mob as a collection of cartoon villains, instead treating it as an accelerating mechanism:

* **Accelerated Emotion:** Rumor outruns verification, shifting seamlessly into absolute certainty, which is then weaponized as a moral duty to punish.
* **Diffusion of Responsibility:** Individuals outsource their personal conscience to the crowd, absolving themselves of guilt while indulging in collective violence.

### II. From Victim to Avenger: The Limits of Procedure

Surviving the arson attack on the jail, Joe Wilson (Spencer Tracy) chooses to remain "dead" to manipulate the legal system into an instrument of lethal revenge:

* The film exposes a double tragedy: what mass violence does to institutional integrity, and how survival fractures the victim's moral core.
* As Stella Bruzzi notes through the framework of the *trial form*, legal mechanisms struggle immensely to process and metabolize dispersed collective guilt.

### III. Historical Catalyst and Studio Constraints

* **Historical Anchor:** The echo of the 1933 Brooke Hart kidnapping case in San Jose, compounded by the shocking political condoning of mob actions by California Governor James Rolph Jr.
* **Production Code Interventions:** Operating under strict Hays Office guidelines, MGM required structural softening—including a final reconciliatory gesture—to temper Lang’s uncompromising indictment of civic morality.

### IV. Conclusion

*Fury* endures as a chilling diagnosis of human behavior. Whenever proof is slow and emotion runs fast, institutions and crowds easily manufacture the "certainty" needed to justify cruelty—making the film's warning as urgent today as it was in 1936.
