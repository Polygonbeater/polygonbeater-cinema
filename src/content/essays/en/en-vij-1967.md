The film adaptation of Nikolai Gogol’s story Viy (1967), directed by Konstantin Yershov and Georgy Kropachyov, represents a unique phenomenon within Eastern Bloc cinema. Although produced in the center of the Soviet film industry (Mosfilm), it opens an unusually broad space for irrationality, folklore, and religious imagination—themes that stood on the margins of official Soviet cultural production. While Viy was not conceived as open political subversion, it utilized the genre of folkloric adaptation to introduce ancient layers of local myth into a strictly materialist environment.

### 1. The Return of the Repressed: The Wooden Church as Autonomous Space
From the perspective of cultural studies, the aesthetics of gothic and horror frequently operate as tools for expressing anxieties repressed by official rationalist discourse. The film’s central act is confined to an old wooden church in the remote Ukrainian countryside. The wooden church is not merely a set piece, but a symbolic territory of local memory, visually and narratively separated from modern institutions and rationalized structures of power. Within this isolated space, chthonic forces (embodied by Pannochka and the monster Viy) remain the defining element of reality.

### 2. Ethnographic Resistance and Gogolian Identity
Although the film communicates in Russian and was produced under the supervision of Moscow studios, its semiotic codes are deeply tied to the Ukrainian folkloric and cultural imagination. The Cossack characters, the specific aesthetics of the village (khutir), and folk demonology constitute a registry that retains significant cultural specificity. As literary scholars point out, Gogol’s early work stands at the intersection of Ukrainian and Russian cultural traditions, and the appropriation of his work is continuously disrupted by this untamable local folklore. The film thus serves as an artifact that, under the patronage of classic literary adaptation, reproduces a distinct folkloric identity.

### 3. The Ambivalence of Rationality and the Survival of Myth
The horror genre held a persistently problematic position in Soviet cinema, as it was frequently associated with "bourgeois" genre conventions, irrationality, and superstition. Viy represented a rare exception that passed through the censorship system primarily due to its status as a classic literary work. However, the film ends with the defeat of the protagonist, the seminary student Khoma Brut, and radically refuses to offer a traditional rationalist explanation for the supernatural events. Although the ending can be read in various ways (from religious allegory to moral failure), from the perspective of cultural memory, the film can be interpreted as a powerful reminder that folkloric imagination survives even in environments striving for ideological control and the rationalization of society.
