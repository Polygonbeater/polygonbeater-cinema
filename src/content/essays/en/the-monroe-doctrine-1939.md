The short film *The Monroe Doctrine* (1939), produced by Warner Bros., serves as a crucial historical artifact, reflecting the United States' deeply entrenched stance of isolationism on the eve of World War II. It is not a critique, but rather a didactic and patriotic defense of America's traditional foreign policy, promoting the idea that the US should focus solely on the Western Hemisphere while avoiding the "entangling alliances" of Europe.

In 1939, as war broke out across the Atlantic, the film aimed to reassure the American public that the nation’s duty was to protect its own sphere of influence—all of the Americas—from hostile European powers (specifically Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy) who were attempting to gain a foothold through economic and political subversion. Critically, even in its inception in 1823, the Doctrine was inherently unilateral and paternalistic. It was not a pact with Latin American nations but a declaration about them, positioning the US as the region's self-appointed guardian and treating other sovereign nations as subjects of American security, rather than partners.

## Critical Reassessment: The Doctrine’s Imperialistic Legacy

While the 1939 film presented the Doctrine as a peaceful shield against foreign influence, its true legacy is defined by its application. Historically, the Doctrine’s original principle of non-intervention in Europe was swiftly inverted to justify rampant interventionism in Latin America.

This shift was decisively cemented by the Roosevelt Corollary (1904), which claimed for the US the right to exercise "an international police power" over Latin American states to prevent European creditors from intervening. This mechanism effectively allowed the US to become the sole economic and military arbiter, transforming the Western Hemisphere into an American 'closed economic space' long before modern trade wars. This established precedent of intervention and economic control formed the practical basis for subsequent US-backed coups and the exploitation of natural resources, defining the Doctrine as a vehicle for American Imperialism.

## The Modern Era: The Critical "Corolla Version"

The term "Corolla Version of the Monroe Doctrine" is an analytical construct used to describe strategies combining traditional geopolitical dominance with modern economic protectionism. This approach represents a calculated effort to dismantle established alliances and treaties in favor of unilateral national interest.

### Distancing from Allies and Undermining Treaties

Modern policies mark a distinct retreat from the post-WWII internationalist consensus. By repeatedly challenging the core commitments of alliances and signalling the intent to withdraw from treaties or agreements, the US signals a profound distancing from traditional partners. This echoes the isolationist sentiment of 1939 but applies it to the very system the US helped create, weakening collective security models.

### Economic Coercion and Sphere Consolidation

The "Corolla" aspect refers to the use of economic tools—specifically the threat of tariffs and border taxes—to enforce manufacturing compliance within the US or its immediate neighbors. This economic coercion is leveraged as a geopolitical weapon to achieve the aims of the Monroe Doctrine: to ensure US economic dominance in the hemisphere and, crucially, to exclude China from strategic investments, such as 5G networks, energy projects, and infrastructure across Latin America.

The core danger of this modern version is that it attempts to re-impose a 19th-century geopolitical framework in a 21st-century multipolar world. While the original doctrine faced competition from declining European powers, the modern version faces peer competition from global economic giants like China. By simultaneously alienating traditional US allies and antagonizing emerging economies in its own hemisphere, this approach risks diminishing US soft power and inadvertently driving Latin American nations toward the non-Western powers it seeks to exclude.
