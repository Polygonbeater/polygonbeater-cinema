This study analyzes Christian Tafdrup's Danish-Dutch film *Speak No Evil* (*Gæsterne*, 2022) and James Watkins's American remake *Speak No Evil* (2024) as two distinct cinematic interpretations of social conformity, passivity, and the failure of interpersonal trust. Both films follow a family that accepts a weekend invitation from an apparently friendly couple and gradually finds itself trapped in a situation in which ordinary rules of social politeness are turned against them.

Methodologically, the study employs comparative audiovisual analysis, focusing primarily on sound design, camera composition, spatial organization, editing, and changes in visual style. While Tafdrup's 2022 original constructs horror primarily through restraint, stasis, and the gradual violation of social boundaries, Watkins's 2024 remake shifts the emphasis toward physical confrontation and active resistance.

Both films can therefore be understood as studies of a mechanism in which politeness ceases to function as a form of mutual protection and becomes an instrument of control. The predators exploit social uncertainty, shame, and hesitation, while the films themselves manipulate the viewer's perception of danger through audiovisual form. The fundamental difference between the two versions therefore lies not only in their narrative resolutions, but also in their contrasting cinematic constructions of passivity and resistance.

### I. The Acoustics of Discomfort and the Weapon of Ambiguity

Both films construct horror through the gradual violation of personal and social boundaries. The process does not begin with direct confrontation, but with a sequence of minor incidents that can initially be interpreted as rudeness, awkwardness, or cultural misunderstanding. Precisely this ambiguity enables the predators to continue their manipulation while simultaneously making it more difficult for their victims to respond immediately.

Sound plays a crucial role in this process.

Tafdrup's original makes particularly prominent use of its musical component as a counterpoint to an apparently civilized visual environment. Sune Kølster's score creates tension that precedes the explicit revelation of danger. The image may present smiles, conversation, and an idyllic setting, while the soundtrack suggests that something fundamentally disturbing exists beneath the surface. Sound therefore functions not merely as accompaniment to the image, but as an independent layer of narrative information.

This produces what can be termed an acoustics of discomfort: the viewer is not always able to articulate precisely what is wrong, yet the soundtrack draws attention to the discrepancy between what is seen and what is felt.

The 2024 remake employs a somewhat different strategy. Diegetic sounds of everyday life become more prominent—sounds associated with eating, pauses in conversation, changes in vocal intensity, or mechanical and motor sounds. Ordinary sounds can thus become carriers of discomfort. This is important because the situation itself remains socially interpretable for a considerable period as merely unpleasant rather than unequivocally dangerous.

The predators in both films exploit precisely this boundary between discomfort and explicit threat. The victim must first determine whether their own reaction is justified or excessive. Social norms therefore create a psychological delay between the perception of danger and the legitimate act of resistance.

In this sense, the films depict a mechanism of social destabilization: the character recognizes that a boundary has been crossed, yet simultaneously doubts whether they have the right to respond to that violation. Politeness ceases to be a neutral social convention and becomes a mechanism for postponing action.

This is one of the most important ways in which the films depart from conventional survival horror. The protagonists are not initially imprisoned physically. They are imprisoned by their interpretation of the situation.

### II. Toxic Masculinity and the Visual Tyranny of the Frame

The American version places greater emphasis on masculinity and on the social expectation that a man should be capable of controlling a threatening situation. Paddy, portrayed by James McAvoy, represents a more overtly physical and dominant form of predatory behavior.

His presence is frequently reinforced through camera composition that emphasizes his physicality and his ability to occupy space. Camera height, central positioning within the frame, and the spatial relationship between individual characters create a visual hierarchy. Paddy therefore becomes dominant not only through dialogue or physical strength, but also through the way his body is positioned within the cinematic frame.

The visiting father, meanwhile, is confronted with an implicit demand to demonstrate his masculine authority. The predator does not offer him fear alone; he also presents a model of how a "real man" is expected to behave. This strategy is significant because the manipulation is not based exclusively on intimidation. Paddy creates an environment in which the victim may feel inadequate precisely because he refuses to accept the predator's rules.

The American remake therefore connects social conformity with the pressure of performative masculinity.

In the Danish original, this mechanism is less physically explicit. The tyranny of the hosts operates primarily through the social environment. The camera frequently maintains a degree of distance, allowing the characters to remain part of a larger spatial configuration. The hosts appear natural within their own domestic environment, while the guests gradually become alien elements within it.

The distinction can therefore be formulated as follows: while the American version emphasizes the dominance of the body, the Danish original emphasizes the dominance of the social situation.

In both films, however, the predator does not compel obedience solely through direct threat. The victim is also offered the possibility of preserving their existing self-image. The victim therefore retreats not only because they are afraid, but also because they want to believe that they remain in control of the situation.

### III. Negative Space versus the Kinetics of the Arena

One of the most significant differences between the two films lies in their treatment of space.

Tafdrup's original repeatedly creates situations in which the characters appear to have sufficient physical space to escape. Wide compositions and the use of negative space can produce a paradoxical sense of openness: the environment is not necessarily physically claustrophobic, yet the characters remain unable to free themselves.

This contradiction can be described as the "open door syndrome." The door may be open and an escape route may be physically available, yet psychological and social mechanisms prevent the characters from using it.

This principle is central to understanding the passivity of the original. The characters are not immediately deprived of their ability to act. Instead, they progressively postpone action. Each new violation of a boundary is rationalized as something that can still be tolerated, explained, or repaired.

Negative space consequently becomes a metaphor for an unused possibility of action.

Watkins's remake gradually reverses this principle. Once the threat is clearly identified, the cinematic language changes as well. In the final section, the image becomes increasingly kinetic, camera movement intensifies, and space ceases to function primarily as a social environment. It becomes a physical arena.

This transition can be interpreted as an audiovisual equivalent of a cognitive breaking point. As long as the characters hesitate, cinematic space remains compatible with social interpretation. Once the threat is recognized as genuine, the need to preserve the illusion of normality disappears, and space becomes a site of immediate survival.

The original therefore revolves around the question:
"Why don't they leave?"
The remake places greater emphasis on:
"What will they do once it is no longer possible to pretend that everything is all right?"

The difference is not merely narrative. It is inscribed directly into cinematic form.

### IV. Two Faces of Violence and the Colors of Doom

Both films ultimately arrive at violence, but their relationship to violence is markedly different.

Tafdrup's original concludes its logic in an extremely nihilistic manner. In the final confrontation, violence is stripped of the conventional function of catharsis. It does not restore order or provide the hero with a triumphant victory. Instead, it represents the consequence of a prolonged series of concessions in which individual boundaries have gradually been moved.

The well-known final line, "Because you let me," therefore functions as a condensation of the film's underlying logic. It should not necessarily be interpreted as assigning responsibility for the violence to the victim. Rather, it expresses the predator's cynical logic: every previous hesitation is appropriated as evidence of the victim's submission.

The visual style of the conclusion reinforces this sense of hopelessness. Violence is not constructed as a triumphant action spectacle. It is presented with a degree of coldness and immediacy that emphasizes its absurdity and finality.

The American remake, by contrast, transforms the conclusion into a substantially more physical and action-oriented sequence. Color, movement, blood, mud, fire, and kinetic camerawork produce a different emotional experience. Violence acquires the function of active resistance and survival.

This distinction is fundamental. In the original, violence is primarily the consequence of a failure to resist. In the remake, violence also becomes a means of recovering the capacity to act.

This does not necessarily mean that the remake simply presents violence as the correct solution. More precisely, it changes its dramatic function. Violence is no longer merely the final stage of helplessness; it becomes a means through which the characters attempt to regain control over their bodies and their physical environment.

### Conclusion

Christian Tafdrup's *Speak No Evil* (2022) and James Watkins's 2024 remake can be read as two different audiovisual studies of the same problem: what happens when social politeness begins to work against the person who attempts to uphold it?

Tafdrup's original develops this problem through passivity, social hesitation, and the gradual erosion of boundaries. Its horror lies not simply in the emergence of predators, but in the inability of the victims to accept their own discomfort as sufficient justification for action. Open space consequently becomes a space of entrapment.

Watkins's remake reformulates the same mechanism. While its first two acts retain the logic of social hesitation, the final section reverses it. Once the illusion of normality definitively collapses, not only the behavior of the characters but also the film's formal language changes. Controlled imagery gives way to movement, social space becomes an arena, and psychological negotiation becomes direct physical confrontation.

The most productive connection between the two films is therefore the concept of a technical architecture of obedience. Sound creates bodily discomfort, framing organizes social hierarchy, space enables or restricts action, and editing determines the rhythm through which both characters and viewers recognize the threat. Cinematic techniques consequently do not merely represent the psychology of the characters; they create the conditions within which their decisions take place.

The "open door syndrome" can thus be understood as the central metaphor of both films. It does not refer solely to the physical doors of the house. It refers to all situations in which a person retains the possibility of leaving, yet social convention suggests that leaving would be impolite, excessive, or inappropriate.

This is where the two films ultimately converge. Their common subject is not simply violence, but the moment at which politeness ceases to function as a mechanism of reciprocity and becomes an obstacle to self-defense.

The principal difference between Tafdrup and Watkins lies in their respective responses to this situation. The original examines the tragic consequences of continually postponing resistance; the remake explores the possibility that a decisive transformation occurs when a character ceases to regard their own defensive response as socially unacceptable.

In both films, cinematic form therefore reinforces the same fundamental observation: the most dangerous moment may not be when the door is locked, but when it is still open and the individual chooses not to walk through it.

### Glossary

* **Open Door Syndrome:** A psychological state where a victim possesses physical avenues of escape from danger, but remains paralyzed socially or mentally, staying in a dangerous environment.
* **Acoustics of Discomfort:** The deliberate use of sound (diegetic or non-diegetic) to induce subliminal anxiety and a sense of threat in the viewer.
* **Community Gaslighting:** A manipulative technique in which an aggressor convinces the victim that their boundary-enforcing reaction is unreasonable or hysterical.
* **Negative Space:** A visual composition technique in cinematography where empty space surrounding characters highlights their isolation, vulnerability, or incapacity to act.
