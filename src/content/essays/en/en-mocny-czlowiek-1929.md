The film *Mocny człowiek*, which premiered on October 2, 1929, simultaneously in two Warsaw cinemas, stands as one of the most important and mature works of Polish silent cinema. Director Henryk Szaro, alongside screenwriter Jerzy Braun, adapted the eponymous 1912 novel by Stanisław Przybyszewski.¹ The creators' goal was to translate the literary theme of guilt, punishment, and morbid ambition into a modern visual language deeply influenced by contemporary German Expressionism.

Contemporary critics and the public received the film with enthusiasm, praising above all its modern editing, the dynamic pacing of the narrative, and the director's exceptional skill. Although *Mocny człowiek* was successfully exported to several European countries after its release, not a single copy survived in Poland following the Second World War, and the work was considered lost for decades. It was not until 1997 that a copy with French subtitles was discovered in the Royal Film Archive in Brussels, subsequently undergoing a full digital reconstruction.²

### 1. Premise and Narrative Structure
The centerpiece of the narrative is the character of a mediocre and talentless journalist, Henryk Bielecki, portrayed by Gregori Chmara. Bielecki yearns for literary fame and wealth but severely lacks the necessary abilities. During a visit to his friend—the talented but terminally ill and morphine-addicted writer Jerzy Górski (played by Artur Socha)—he reads Górski's new manuscript. Although Bielecki is fully aware of the work's immense artistic value, he maliciously dismisses it as subpar in front of the author, entirely crushing his friend's hopes. Immediately afterward, he offers Górski morphine, knowingly pushing him toward suicide.

Under the cover of night, Bielecki returns to Górski's apartment and steals the manuscript, unaware that he is being observed from behind a curtain by his own mistress, Łucja (Agnes Kuck). Bielecki had previously rejected her, and she had come to the dying Górski seeking solace. Bielecki shamelessly publishes the stolen novel, titled *Mocny człowiek*, under his own name. He instantly achieves his desired recognition, becomes the darling of high society, and his face fills the front pages of newspapers.³

### 2. The Theme of Guilt and Punishment
However, the central conflict is not exhausted by the crime itself. The story fractures the moment Bielecki meets Nina Ligęza (Maria Majdrowicz), a married woman for whom he develops profound feelings. Paradoxically, his love for another woman awakens his hitherto silenced conscience. Bielecki, celebrated by society as a literary genius, gradually succumbs to the crushing weight of his own deceit.

The secret known to his abandoned mistress Łucja, coupled with his own rapidly deepening paranoia, creates an unbearable psychological pressure. *Mocny człowiek* thus unfolds as a tragic parable of a man who, in order to achieve the status of a so-called Übermensch, stops at no crime, only to be ultimately destroyed by the very success that was never rightfully his.
