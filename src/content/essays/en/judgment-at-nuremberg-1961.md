> *“Evil is presented not as the result of fanaticism, but as the result of societal consent and silence.”*

Set in 1948, Stanley Kramer’s *Judgment at Nuremberg* (1961) depicts one of the subsequent Nuremberg trials—specifically prosecuting former German judges and prosecutors who upheld a system of legal lawlessness (*Gesetzliches Unrecht*) under the Nazi regime. American Judge Dan Haywood (Spencer Tracy) must determine whether these men bear personal moral guilt or were mere enforcers of state law.

## The Theme of Collective Guilt

Kramer's film transcends the courtroom to ask a universal question: *Is individual guilt possible when evil is institutionalized?*

* **Documentary Reality:** The screening of authentic concentration camp footage dissolves the boundary between fiction and reality, transferring guilt from the defendants to all who remained silent or excused themselves with necessity.
* **Geopolitical Compromise:** Judge Haywood faces pressure from American officials eager to conclude the trial due to the burgeoning Cold War, exposing the moral compromises of the victors.

> *“Radbruch’s Formula: statutory law that is intolerable must yield to true justice.”*

## Cinematic Composition and Dialogue

The static camera and long takes evoke the relentlessness of a tribunal of conscience. Horizontal compositions create a rigid structure of power from which there is no escape. Maximilian Schell’s Oscar-winning defense attorney Hans Rolfe stands as the voice of rationalized complicity, echoing Hannah Arendt's concepts on the banality of evil.
