Lokis (1970), directed by Polish filmmaker Janusz Majewski and adapted from Prosper Mérimée's novella, represents a highly sophisticated contribution to Eastern European cinematic gothic. Set in the 19th century on a remote estate in the Lithuanian region of Samogitia, the plot centers on the classic horror motif of lycanthropy (here associated with a bear). Within the context of cultural geography and memory studies, however, the film functions primarily as a fascinating analysis of the limits of Western rationalism when confronted with deeply rooted local mythology. The film can be read as an exploration of the limits of rational understanding when confronted with cultural otherness.

### 1. The Ethnographer as a Castaway of Reason
The protagonist, Pastor and linguist Wittenbach, arrives at the Lithuanian estate as a representative of educated 19th-century European rationalism. His goal is to study ancient texts, systematize the local language, and categorize folklore into rational, scholarly frameworks. The film gradually depicts the disintegration of this methodology. Wittenbach's analytical apparatus fails in the face of Count Szemiot's dark secret and the wildness of local traditions. From the perspective of cinematic interpretation, Lokis does not present folklore merely as an object of study, but as a consuming force that ultimately paralyzes the observational detachment of the Enlightenment intellectual.

### 2. Samogitia as a Zone of Otherness
The space of the borderland Lithuanian forests serves not merely as an atmospheric backdrop, but assumes the role of an autonomous actor within the narrative. The forests, swamps, and folk customs of the local inhabitants construct an environment in which pagan cults intertwine with Christian mysticism and natural cycles. On a symbolic level, this zone operates as a dual periphery: it exists on the margins of both the Russian Empire of the time and Western rational Europe. The film visualizes a space that resists external classification, a realm where ancient chthonic forces—symbolically embodied by the bear's curse—continue to shape the way the local world interprets itself.

### 3. The Subversion of the Rationalizing Narrative
Unlike classic Western gothic tales (such as Dracula), where representatives of the modern world ultimately combine reason and science to defeat or explain the threat, Lokis culminates in a catastrophe that Wittenbach is unable to prevent or satisfactorily explain. The film skillfully maintains narrative ambivalence between psychological deviation and supernatural curse. Although the film cannot primarily be viewed as a political allegory, on the level of cultural history, it offers a powerful narrative: it serves as a reminder that the local, pre-modern layers of Eastern European memory and mythology maintain their resistance against attempts at definitive rationalization and scientific assimilation.
