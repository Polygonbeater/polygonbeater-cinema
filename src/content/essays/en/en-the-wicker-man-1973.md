This study deconstructs the foundational work of the folk horror subgenre, *The Wicker Man* (1973, running time 88 minutes), as a radical polemic against the concept of civilizational superiority and religious dogma. The film analyzes a puritanical police sergeant's search for a missing girl on the isolated island of Summerisle, where the inhabitants practice pre-Christian Celtic polytheism. 

Methodologically, the text focuses on the phenomenon of "daylight horror," the diegetic use of Paul Giovanni's folk music, and the inevitable clash of two irreconcilable ideological verticals. The film demonstrates that the purest form of terror does not hide in the shadows, but arrives with a smile, a song on the lips, and in broad daylight.

### I. The Horizontal of Daylight and the False Idyll

The visual identity of the film presents an absolute inversion of traditional horror rules. Director Robin Hardy and screenwriter Anthony Shaffer reject Gothic props (darkness, fog, crumbling castles). The evil in *The Wicker Man* operates on the sun-drenched horizontal of a blooming spring landscape. 

The island of Summerisle appears as a pastoral utopia where inhabitants live in complete harmony with nature, celebrate sexuality, and view death as a natural part of the cycle. This visual and atmospheric idyll, however, is merely a perfect psychological trap. The viewer, like the protagonist, is blinded by the light and warmth of the community, preventing them from recognizing the cold and relentless logic of the approaching ritual.

### II. The Soundscape as a Tool of Isolation

The music of Paul Giovanni is not mere background in this film; it is a weapon. The songs are an organic part of the plot (diegetic music)—characters sing them in the pub, on the village green, and during rituals. Instead of inducing cheap jump scares, the music serves to deepen the isolation of Sergeant Howie (Edward Woodward). 

Every choral song of the villagers forms an invisible, impenetrable wall that ostracizes the outsider. The music functions here as the sonic manifestation of the island's collective mind, against which the rationality of the individual stands no chance. The children singing about the Maypole or the seductive song of Willow (Britt Ekland) blur the lines between innocence and perversion.

### III. The Clash of Verticals: The Cross vs. Nature

The narrative core of the film is not a battle between good and evil, but the deadly collision of two absolute dogmas. Sergeant Howie represents the rigid Christian vertical and state authority. His pride and unwavering belief in his own moral and civilizational superiority blind him. 

On the other side stands Lord Summerisle (Christopher Lee), an aristocratic intellectual who pragmatically uses paganism to maintain social harmony and the economic prosperity of the island (the apple harvest). Howie's downfall does not lie in moral corruption, but in the fact that his Christian martyr and savior complex fits perfectly into the role the pagans have prepared for him within their sacrificial ritual.

### IV. Conclusion: The Burning Wicker Man and the Deafness of Heaven

The final sequence inside the gigantic wicker statue is one of the most terrifying yet beautiful images in world cinema. Hardy shows the triumph of absolute fanaticism on both sides. 

While the islanders joyfully and with sincere faith sing the Middle English spring song *Sumer Is Icumen In*, the dying Howie desperately recites Psalm 23 into the flames and prays to Christ. Neither dogma yields. There is no "evil" antagonist here, only two groups of people doing what they believe to be absolutely right. *The Wicker Man* leaves the viewer at a point of absolute ontological horror—with the realization that humanity's most horrific acts are often committed with complete conviction of their necessity and sanctity.

### Glossary and Bibliography

* **Daylight Terror:** A subgeneric convention shifting the locus of dread from dark Gothic environments to a sun-drenched, seemingly idyllic rural landscape.
* **Diegetic Isolation through Music:** The strategic deployment of traditional folk songs sung by on-screen characters to build a sound barrier defining collective identity.
* **Clash of Ideological Verticals:** An irreconcilable conflict between two absolute dogmas where pride and moral superiority guarantee a tragic sacrifice.
* **Ontological Horror:** A profound existential dread arising from the realization that humanity's worst atrocities are often committed with sincere holy conviction.
