In A Short Film About Killing (Krótki film o zabijaniu, 1988), Krzysztof Kieślowski strips the act of murder of any genre, romantic, or cathartic veneer. It is neither a horror film nor a conventional thriller. It is a cold, methodical study of the immense physical and institutional effort required to end a human life. The film portrays brutality not as an excess, but as a banal, exhausting, and dirty process.

### The Physics of Strangulation
The centerpiece of the film is the murder of a taxi driver committed by a young man named Jacek. In classical cinema, death lasts mere seconds and often carries an aesthetic or moral value. However, Kieślowski and cinematographer Sławomir Idziak stretch this scene into an exceptionally long and agonizing sequence, ranking among the most discussed scenes of violence in 1980s European cinema.

This scene can be analyzed as a deliberate destruction of the cinematic myth of death. Killing here is not an act of strength, but a clumsy, chaotic, and exhausting process. Jacek strangles and beats the taxi driver, and when the victim continues to breathe, he must use a rock. The film forces us to observe the very mechanics of dying, the body’s animalistic resistance, and the horrifying banality of blood and grime. The brutality lies not in the gore, but in the unbearable duration and physical weight of the act.

### The Symmetry of Violence and the Coldness of the State
Kieślowski’s film is built around the parallel trajectories of three characters (Jacek, the taxi driver, and the young defense attorney Piotr), whose fates gradually converge into a chilling symmetry. Jacek’s chaotic murder is followed by a second murder—the state’s execution of Jacek. From a sociological and legal framework, the film can be read as a devastating critique of capital punishment.

The state machinery is depicted with terrifying meticulousness. The preparation of the noose, the testing of the trapdoor mechanism, the bureaucratic procedures, and the presence of a doctor form an institutional mirror to Jacek’s crime. The film deliberately juxtaposes both acts, inviting the viewer to question whether there is a fundamental moral difference between a private murder and a clinical state execution. The brutality of the institution is all the more terrifying because it is cloaked in the mantle of justice.

### Visual Asphyxiation
A crucial element of the film is its technical and visual execution. Sławomir Idziak used a system of custom-modified green and yellow filters over the lens. The result is an image that looks diseased. The sky is not blue, but a toxic yellow-green; people’s faces appear unhealthy, and the edges of the frames are often covered in dark vignetting.

This cinematographic approach serves not merely to create a "dirty" atmosphere. It can be interpreted as the visualization of the social and moral disintegration of late-socialist Warsaw. The world in the film looks as though it is suffocating, long before the first noose is ever tightened. Idziak’s camerawork permits the viewer no escape; every composition functions as a claustrophobic trap.

### Conclusion: Death Without Glorification
A Short Film About Killing is a work that physically exhausts the viewer. It leaves no room for pity, makes no excuses for the killer, and offers no absolution to the state. It is the ultimate demystification of death. It demonstrates that killing a human being—whether in the mud on the outskirts of a city or in a tile-lined execution chamber—is always a physical interruption of human life, stripped of any glorification or romanticization. It is brutality stripped to the bone.
