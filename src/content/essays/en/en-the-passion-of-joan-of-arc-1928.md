This study deconstructs the silent era masterpiece *The Passion of Joan of Arc* (1928, running time 82 minutes) as a radical exploration of the limits of the cinematic medium and human endurance. The film analyzes the trial of Joan of Arc not as a historical epic, but as an intimate, claustrophobic clash between earthly dogmatism and pure spiritual faith. 

Methodologically, the text focuses on the revolutionary use of extreme close-ups (cinematographer Rudolph Maté), the conscious disorientation of spatial awareness, and the iconic, naturalistic performance of Renée Jeanne Falconetti. The film demonstrates that the most brutal conflict does not occur on a physical battlefield, but within the microcosm of the human face, where institutional power is utterly powerless against inner conviction.

### I. The Face as the Sole Landscape

The visual identity of the film represents a ground zero in the history of cinema. Dreyer and his cinematographer Rudolph Maté almost entirely eliminated establishing shots. They stripped the viewer of the safety of spatial orientation, replacing the physical world with an endless succession of extreme close-ups. 

The face of Renée Jeanne Falconetti becomes the only true landscape of the film—a topography of suffering where every wrinkle, pore, and tear is visible. The director's strict refusal of any film makeup tore the theatrical masks off the actors, transforming the image into a raw, almost clinically precise record of the human soul in an extreme situation.

### II. The Geometry of Oppression and the Direction of the Gaze

The clash between the earthly world and the spiritual sphere is expressed purely through camera angles and the direction of the characters' gazes. The inquisitors represent the crushing weight of institution and power; they are frequently shot from extreme low angles, visually distorting them and turning them into giant, menacing monuments towering over their victim. 

Their gazes point uncompromisingly downward, toward the earth and the physical body. Conversely, Joan is shot from slight high angles to emphasize her physical vulnerability and isolation, yet her eyes are constantly fixed upward, beyond the frame of the image. Her gaze pierces the ceiling of the courtroom, directed toward a transcendence that secular power cannot reach.

### III. Claustrophobia and the Asymmetry of Power

The production design by Hermann Warm and Jean Hugo is intentionally abstract and bare. The windowless white walls and broken, asymmetrical arches do not create a historical illusion of the 15th century, but rather an architectural manifestation of psychological terror. 

Dreyer's aggressive, jump-cut editing (anticipating modern cinema) builds a frenetic rhythm in which the faces of the inquisitors attack Joan like visual blows. There is no background into which the heroine or the viewer can escape; the space is defined exclusively by the pressure it exerts on the center.

### IV. Conclusion: Fire as an Ontological Victory

The final execution sequence at the stake radically subverts the logic of defeat. The physical destruction of Joan's body in the flames is not the triumph of her judges, but her ultimate liberation. The fire consumes the earthly vessel, thereby enabling the absolute victory of her spirit. 

The subsequent riot by the observing crowd and its brutal suppression by the military then pulls the film back into raw reality—demonstrating that once the spiritual absolute is revealed in the material world, it incites chaos, for a corrupted system cannot bear the mirror of its own brutality.

### Glossary and Bibliography

* **The Face as Sole Landscape:** An aesthetic strategy eliminating establishing shots in favor of extreme close-ups of human expression devoid of makeup.
* **Geometry of Oppression:** The strategic use of contrasting high and low camera angles to map unbalanced power dynamics.
* **Abstract Set Design:** Minimalist, asymmetrical architectural spaces designed as physical manifestations of institutional terror.
* **Ontological Victory:** The reframing of physical destruction at the stake as ultimate spiritual liberation and triumph over dogma.
