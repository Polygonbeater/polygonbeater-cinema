This study deconstructs Akira Kurosawa’s film *Rashomon* (1950, running time 88 min) as a pivotal work of modern cinema that introduced non-linear storytelling based on subjectivity. The film won the Golden Lion at the Venice Film Festival in 1951 and introduced the term "Rashomon effect" into the English language and scientific discourse. 

The film analyzes a single violent act (the murder of a samurai and the rape of his wife) through four mutually exclusive testimonies. Methodologically, the text relies on the analysis of Kazuo Miyagawa’s camera kinetics, the study of unreliable narrators, and the socio-psychological impact of the film on the perception of individual integrity in a fractured world.

### I. The Forest as a Labyrinth of the Psyche

The visual style of the film is defined by revolutionary work with natural light. Cinematographer Kazuo Miyagawa pointed the lens directly at the sun for the first time in history, creating a "flickering truth" effect through dense tree canopies. 

The forest does not function as a mere backdrop but as a claustrophobic space where characters lose themselves in their own versions of events. The ruins of the Rashomon gate in the lashing rain symbolize the total collapse of the moral order.

### II. Four Versions: Ego as a Narrative Engine

The film presents the event through four perspectives, where each character modifies the facts to preserve their self-image (ego):

* **The Bandit Tajomaru (Toshiro Mifune):** Styles himself as a heroic conqueror. His version is a celebration of his own strength and masculinity.
* **The Wife (Machiko Kyo):** Presents herself as a betrayed and suffering victim, attempting to evoke sympathy and justify her actions.
* **The Samurai (Masayuki Mori):** Speaks through a medium. Even in death, he lies to preserve his honor (presenting his death as suicide instead of defeat).
* **The Woodcutter (Takashi Shimura):** A seemingly objective observer who also lies to cover up his own minor moral failure and the theft of a valuable dagger.

### III. Conclusion: Ethical Act in an Epistemological Vacuum

Kurosawa is not searching for the "actual" truth in a procedural sense. The core of the film is the realization that people are incapable of truth if it threatens their ego. 

The only redemption from this vacuum is the final acceptance of the foundling by the woodcutter (Takashi Shimura). This act is not an intellectual solution to the truth but a practical ethical deed—a return to humanity despite the universal lie.

### Glossary and Bibliography

* **The Rashomon Effect:** An epistemological phenomenon where an identical event is interpreted in radically different and contradictory ways by different witnesses.
* **Unreliable Narrator:** A storytelling device where character testimonies are warped by self-deception, ego preservation, and social vanity.
* **Kazuo Miyagawa's Cinematography:** Revolutionary camera work pointing the lens directly into natural sunlight through forest vegetation to mirror fractured perception.
* **Epistemological Relativism:** The philosophical framework suggesting that objective truth is either inaccessible or entirely constructed by subjective self-interest.
