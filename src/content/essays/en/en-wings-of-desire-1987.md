This study deconstructs Wim Wenders' poetic masterpiece *Wings of Desire* (Der Himmel über Berlin, 1987, running time 128 minutes) as the absolute visualization of the ontological intersection between spirit and matter. The film analyzes the existence of angels who wander as silent witnesses through divided Berlin, incapable of physical experience. 

Methodologically, the text focuses on the duality of Henri Alekan's cinematography (black-and-white eternity vs. color mortality), Peter Handke's poetic text, and the spatial metaphor of the Berlin Wall. The film demonstrates a radical philosophical turn: the perfection of the vertical (immortality) is empty without the pain, weight, and love offered by the human horizontal.

### I. Black and White Eternity and the Color of Time

The visual dialectic of the film is a masterclass by cinematographer Henri Alekan. The angels' perspective is captured in monochromatic, black-and-white tones. This visual coldness does not represent sorrow, but absolute, objective eternity—a state without time, without friction, and without physical limits. 

Only when the film switches to human subjective perception (or when the angel Damiel loses his immortality) does the screen explode into color. Color here is not merely an aesthetic addition but ontological proof of linear time, blood, and mortality.

### II. The Verticality of Angels and the Horizontal of the Wall

The spatial geometry of the film is the precise definition of the cross. The angels Damiel (Bruno Ganz) and Cassiel (Otto Sander) move along the vertical: they stand atop churches, on the Victory Column, walk through walls, and ignore physical boundaries. 

Below them, however, lies Berlin—a city brutally severed by the Wall, which forms an absolute barrier on the horizontal axis of human lives. The angels hear the cacophony of human thoughts and see the pain of isolation, but they cannot intervene. Their transcendence gradually becomes a prison rather than a privilege.

### III. The Circus and the Desire for Gravity

Damiel's transformation is initiated by two key encounters. The first is the trapeze artist Marion (Solveig Dommartin), whose existence in the circus represents a perfect play with gravity—she falls and rises again, embodying both physical weight and beauty. 

The second is actor Peter Falk (playing himself), a former angel who conveys to Damiel the small, tangible joys of existence: the warmth of coffee, rubbing hands together, the feeling of cold. Falk's monologues deconstruct the myth of the spirit's superiority over matter.

### IV. Conclusion: Incarnation and the Acceptance of Blood

In the conclusion, Damiel chooses to fall. He surrenders immortality to feel the weight of his own body and to see the blood from a wound he sustains immediately after his "incarnation." This moment is not a tragic loss (like the fall of the angels in Milton's *Paradise Lost*), but a triumph. 

Damiel's journey to Marion represents the perfect synthesis: the vertical voluntarily descended to the horizontal to find meaning within it. Wenders thus created the deepest existential thesis—the purpose of human existence is not an escape into perfect eternity, but the full embrace of our limited, vulnerable earthly nature.

### Glossary and Bibliography

* **Black-and-White Eternity vs. Color Mortality:** The visual duality of Henri Alekan's cinematography contrasting the timeless perception of immortal beings with the colorful, linear experience of mortals.
* **The Vertical of Transcendence and Horizontal of the Wall:** The spatial geometry contrasting the angels' uninhibited spiritual mobility with the physical division of human existence.
* **Desire for Gravity:** The philosophical shift from pure spiritual detachment to physical embodiment, vulnerability, touch, and weight.
* **Incarnation as Triumph:** Reframing the angel's fall not as a tragic loss or punishment, but as a conscious choice and the ultimate fulfillment of human existence.
