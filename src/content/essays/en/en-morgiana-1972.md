There are films that look back at the spectator as intensely as the spectator looks at them. Juraj Herz’s Morgiana (1972) is not merely a story of two sisters, played by Iva Janžurová; it is a visual opera of narcissism, unfolding in an interior of mirrors that reflect only emptiness. While other auteurs perceive evil as an external contagion or a political system, Herz presents evil here as an aesthetic choice born from human smallness.

### The Sister as an Alien Object
The relationship between Klára and Victoria is stripped of all sentimentality. Victoria is not a creature of flesh and blood; she is an architecturally perfect predator. Herz gradually strips away the psychological origins of her actions—inheritance, jealousy over beauty or men—until the point where her motivation ceases to be a mere grievance and transforms into an autonomous principle of destruction.

Her power over Klára does not stem from simple envy, but from the total colonization of the other’s reality. Victoria does not act; she directs. Slowly, with surgical precision, she poisons Klára’s environment, her memories, and her senses. This "domestic terror" carries a chilling elegance. Herz confronts us with the worst possible scenario of intimacy: the realization that the person who knows us best is the one who has long since mapped out our extinction.

### The Mirror as a Trap
The visual style of Morgiana is not merely brilliant craftsmanship; it is a trap. Jaroslav Kučera does not use light to reveal, but to distort. Herz repeatedly utilizes mirrors and reflections as a visual motif of disrupted identity and the mutual obsession of the sisters. Each mirror is a tool that shatters identity—while Klára seeks confirmation of her existence in reflections, Victoria uses them as control panels for her callous scheme.

Herz’s world is claustrophobic not because it is small, but because it is too perfect. It is an aestheticized decay. The house itself—from the velvet curtains to the polished silverware—seems complicit in a quiet, rhythmic slaughter. The architecture is not a backdrop; it is a silent companion sharing the secret with Victoria as to why one sister must be erased so the other can shine in the vacuum.

### Evil as an Absolute Process
What sets Morgiana apart from the currents of contemporary Eastern European cinema is the way Herz transforms jealousy into a pure, cold force. Victoria does not need a political program to be terrifying; her own capacity to turn a human being into an art object of ruin is sufficient.

Herz delivers a report on a human being who has decided to be a god in her own microcosm. It is a disturbing gaze into an abyss where morality appears as a useless, archaic habit. Morgiana is a film about absolute solitude. And about the fact that the greatest horror does not wait outside the door in the guise of monsters, but at the table, in the form of a sister who smiles at you and hands you a glass of wine in which your existence is slowly dissolving.
