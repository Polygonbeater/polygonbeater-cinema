When Polish director Henryk Szaro decided to adapt the legend of Master Twardowski in 1936, he was not aiming for a modest, intimate experiment. His goal was to showcase the power and grandeur of Polish cinema. *Pan Twardowski* was one of the most expensive and ambitious projects of its time. Szaro took the ancient tale of the Polish Faust and forged it into a mesmerizing historical epic, blending Renaissance splendor with folk tales of devils and sorcery.

### 1. The Slavic Faust Doesn't Just Want Wisdom; He Wants to Live
Forget the German Faust, agonizing over books in his dusty study. Jan Twardowski (Franciszek Brodniewicz) is a quintessential Polish nobleman—a Sarmatian. He is proud, passionate, and desires everything the world has to offer: wealth, beauty, and infinite power. When he summons dark forces in the Kraków underground, he does so not out of scientific curiosity, but out of sheer, unbridled lust for life.

Moreover, the pact with hell is signed with a characteristic swagger. Twardowski believes he is smarter than the ruler of the underworld himself. He insists on an absurd loophole in the blood-stained parchment: the devil (played with incredibly chilling elegance by Kazimierz Junosza-Stępowski) may only claim his soul in the city of Rome. Naturally, the alchemist has no intention of ever traveling there.¹

### 2. The Illusionist at the Royal Court
While the first half of the film charts Twardowski's rise to power, the second half delivers a visual feast of special effects. The most legendary sequence takes place at the court of the grieving King Sigismund II Augustus. The King implores the sorcerer to grant him one last glimpse of his deceased beloved wife, Barbara Radziwiłłówna. Szaro excels in this scene. Utilizing what were then innovative double exposures and mirror tricks, he creates a beautiful yet terrifying spiritual seance on screen that retains its haunting impact to this day.²

### 3. The Hellish Punchline by the Roadside
The story's climax is a perfect example of folklore's sense of irony. Evil does not need to use brute force; human carelessness is enough. Twardowski, on the run, seeks refuge in a remote country inn. When Mephistopheles suddenly appears before him, the alchemist laughs him off, stating they are not in Italy. The devil merely smiles and points to the peeling wooden sign above the door. The inn is named "Rome" (Rzym).

Although the original legend ends with the devil abducting the alchemist—who is then dropped midway and left trapped on the Moon—the 1930s film offers a more romantic resolution. Even so, *Pan Twardowski* remains a magnificent reminder of an era when filmmakers could mix black magic, historical epics, and national myths into one utterly captivating cinematic experience.³
