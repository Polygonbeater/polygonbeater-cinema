*An Optical Poem*, created by the pioneer of abstract animation Oskar Fischinger and released by Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer (MGM) in 1937, represents a critical moment in the history of visual music. Completed shortly after Fischinger fled Nazi Germany and the purge of "Degenerate Art," this short film is not merely an artistic statement but a powerful demonstration of the freedom of abstract expression denied by the totalitarian regime.

## The Synthesis of Sight and Sound

* **Visual Music:** Fischinger’s work belongs to the school of visual music—the technique of creating dynamic abstract imagery precisely synchronized with an underlying score. In *An Optical Poem*, he uses Franz Liszt’s *Hungarian Rhapsody No. 2*, which dictates the pace, color, and intensity of the visual rhythm.
* **Methodology:** The film was executed using the stop-motion cut-out technique. Unlike hand-drawn animation, Fischinger used thousands of precise geometric shapes (colored circles, triangles, and lines) meticulously placed and photographed frame by frame.
* **The Power of Color:** Shapes constantly dissolve, multiply, and reform, utilizing color and motion to evoke emotion and structure rather than relying on recognizable narrative figures.

## Context: A Triumph Over Tyranny

* **Contrast to Propaganda:** While animation in Germany and the U.S. was rapidly being converted into tools for political propaganda, Fischinger used his art to assert the value of non-ideological, pure artistic creation.
* **The Ultimate Rebuttal:** By achieving mainstream success in Hollywood (MGM distributed the film nationally) with a piece of non-representational art, *An Optical Poem* served as the ultimate rebuttal to the "Entartete Kunst" label, proving that abstract works deemed "degenerate" were in fact a valuable and marketable form of high art.

*An Optical Poem* is an essential film for understanding the artistic migration of the 1930s and the crucial role abstract cinema played in asserting aesthetic liberty against ideological repression.
