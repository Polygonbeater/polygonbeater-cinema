> *“Every act of domination creates its rebellion, and every rebellion risks becoming a new dominion.”*

Released only four years after Algerian independence, Gillo Pontecorvo’s *The Battle of Algiers* (*La battaglia di Algeri*, 1966) reconstructs the Algerian revolution with shocking immediacy. Working alongside former FLN leader Yacef Saadi, Pontecorvo forged a film that transcends propaganda to become a sociological experiment — an anatomy of how violence is born from oppression.

## Realism as Political Language

Shot in a neorealist style with non-professional actors (save for Jean Martin as Mathieu) and hand-held cameras, the film blurs the line between documentary and fiction. 

* **Urban Guerrilla Warfare:** The labyrinth of the Casbah serves as protagonist. The film contrasts the FLN's decentralized cellular pyramid with Colonel Mathieu’s data-driven counter-insurgency and systematic use of torture.
* **The Paradox of Liberation:** Both militants and paratroopers mirror each other in method and conviction. The oppressed adopt the tools of the oppressor, turning liberation into a cycle of destruction.

> *“The French won the tactical battle through torture, but lost the moral and political war. An idea cannot be tortured or killed.”*

## Morricone and Pontecorvo’s Score

The score fuses percussive militaristic snare drums—representing imperial order and the pulse of the Casbah—with Ennio Morricone’s elegiac laments. The music acts as a moral compass, mourning the human cost beneath ideological conflict.
