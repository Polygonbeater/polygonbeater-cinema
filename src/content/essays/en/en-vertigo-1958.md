This study deconstructs Alfred Hitchcock's masterpiece *Vertigo* (1958, running time 128 minutes) as a radical exploration of psychological obsession and manipulation. The film analyzes the destructive power of the "male gaze" through the character of detective Scottie Ferguson, who attempts to remold a living woman into the image of a dead ideal. 

Methodologically, the text focuses on the visual motif of the spiral, the revolutionary use of the "dolly zoom" (vertigo effect) by cinematographer Robert Burks, and the hypnotic score by Bernard Herrmann. The film examines how love can mutate into a necrophilic fixation and how we create illusions that ultimately destroy us.

### I. Topography of Memory and the Visual Spiral

The film's visual structure is built upon the motif of the spiral, introduced in Saul Bass's opening titles and permeating the entire work (the tower stairs, the bun in the hair, the tree rings of the sequoia). 

Cinematographer Robert Burks utilized the dolly zoom technique (a combination of camera movement and opposing zoom) for the first time in cinematic history, visualizing Scottie's vertigo not merely as a physical fear of heights, but as an existential fall into the depths of his own subconscious. San Francisco functions here not as a real city, but as a dreamlike, topographical map of Scottie's delirium.

### II. Scottie and Madeleine: Constructing the Perfect Illusion

The first half of the film unfolds as a traditional ghost story. Scottie (James Stewart, playing sharply against his typical American idealism) falls in love with Madeleine (Kim Novak). 

Hitchcock explores the essence of cinematic illusion here—Scottie does not fall in love with a real woman, but with a carefully constructed image, a performance. Madeleine is a work of art, a mystery designed to entrap the male desire to rescue and control. Her presumed death mentally paralyzes Scottie because he loses not just a woman, but his perfect fantasy.

### III. Judy and the Pygmalion Syndrome

In the second half, the film radically shifts perspective, becoming a cruel psychological autopsy. When Scottie meets Judy (again Kim Novak), he ruthlessly begins remolding her back into the image of the dead Madeleine. 

Hitchcock exposes the toxicity of the male gaze—Scottie is indifferent to Judy's identity; he demands only a physical vessel for his fetishistic obsession. The scene where Judy emerges from the bathroom bathed in green neon light is not a romantic reunion, but the visualization of resurrecting a ghost through psychological violation and the erasure of a real personality.

### IV. Conclusion: Fall into the Abyss of Truth

The final revelation at the tower of Mission San Juan Bautista is not a triumph of justice, but an ultimate disintegration. Scottie is cured of his vertigo the moment he uncovers the truth of the conspiracy, but this truth costs him everything. 

The sudden appearance of a nun (symbolizing the relentless intervention of reality or fate) leads to Judy's fatal fall. Scottie is left standing on the edge of the abyss—cured of his physical vertigo, but permanently destroyed by his psychological one. He survives, but loses the ability to live in a world stripped of illusions.

### Glossary and Bibliography

* **Male Gaze:** Laura Mulvey's theoretical concept describing cinematic visual pleasure, where women are objectified and reshaped according to the controlling desires of the male subject.
* **Dolly Zoom (Vertigo Effect):** A cinematic technique combining backward camera movement with simultaneous zoom-in (or vice versa), creating spatial distortion and visual vertigo.
* **Spiral Motif:** A recurring visual and thematic symbol (titles, stairs, hairstyle) representing an endless fall into the subconscious and obsessive circularity.
* **Pygmalion Syndrome:** A psychological and narrative dynamic in which a male protagonist ruthlessly remolds a living woman into an idealized or dead model.
