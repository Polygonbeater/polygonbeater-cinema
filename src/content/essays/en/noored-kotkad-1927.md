The 1927 silent film Noored kotkad (Young Eagles), directed by Theodor Luts, is one of the most vital surviving works of interwar Estonian cinema. However, it is not merely a foundational war drama; it is, above all, a key visual source of contemporary national imagination, reflecting how the Baltic region wished to see itself before the Soviet annexation in 1940. Celebrating the Estonian War of Independence (1918–1920), the film presents the image of a modern, self-assured European nation that fought for its freedom amidst the ruins of a collapsing empire.

### 1. European Modernity Over Soviet Avant-Garde
Theodor Luts, a pioneer of Estonian filmmaking, did not study cinematography and directing in Moscow; he drew his inspiration from Paris and Berlin. This Western influence is clearly visible in the film. The aesthetic of Young Eagles has nothing in common with the emerging Soviet montage school, which had begun to subordinate film to the ideology of class struggle. Luts’s film is structured as a classic European epic narrative. It follows three young men—a student, a blacksmith, and a farmer—who transcend their social differences not for a class revolution, but to defend national sovereignty. Thus, the film serves as a compelling demonstration that the Estonian creative elite saw its organic development within a Central European and Nordic context.

### 2. Russia as an Invading Force and the Defense of Statehood
From the perspective of decolonizing the cultural narrative, the central conflict of the film is crucial. Soviet and, later, Russian propaganda frequently portrayed its presence in the Baltics as a liberating or civilizing mission. Young Eagles, however, presents the contemporary perspective of the defending state: Russian Bolshevik troops (alongside other hostile historical factions) are depicted exclusively as an invading, destructive force. For a 1927 audience, the film was a celebration of a recent triumph of national self-determination; for today’s viewer, the work serves as a chilling reminder of the region's deep historical experience with Russian imperialism.

### 3. An Archival Weapon Against Memory Erasure
When the Soviet Union annexed Estonia in 1940, one of its first steps was the eradication of independent culture and an attempt to rewrite national memory. Films like Noored kotkad were systematically marginalized, censored, or withdrawn from distribution because their very existence disrupted the Soviet historical narrative. Today, the restored version of the film acts as a time capsule of the First Estonian Republic. It is a visual monument to a time when the Baltics breathed freely, and simultaneously serves as a powerful counterpoint to the imperial myth that these nations owe their development to Moscow.
