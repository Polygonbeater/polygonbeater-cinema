In his sci-fi film O-Bi, O-Ba: The End of Civilization (1985), Piotr Szulkin abandons the traditional genre conventions of post-apocalyptic cinema. He focuses not on the physical struggle for survival in a wasteland, but on the terminal phase of a human society confined within a decaying underground bunker (the Dome) following a nuclear war. The film operates as a chilling sociological study, examining how institutional power utilizes myth to maintain control over a population that realistically has no future.

### The Ark as an Instrument of Power
The central motif of the narrative is the myth of the "Ark"—a rescue ship destined to save the inhabitants of the Dome. However, the film gradually reveals that the Ark is more of a propagandistic construct than a realistic possibility of salvation. Szulkin demonstrates that the Ark functions primarily as a myth of power maintained by the ruling structures. It is a psychological shock absorber intended to prevent anarchy and provide the people with an illusion of purpose.

This construct can be interpreted as a sharp allegory for late-stage totalitarianism (specifically, Polish real socialism of the 1980s). The system can no longer provide basic material needs, the Dome is physically collapsing, and so it circulates an illusion. Szulkin presents hope not as a positive human virtue, but as the ultimate opiate—a paralyzing tool that prevents rebellion and keeps the masses in passive agony.

### Visual Entropy and Claustrophobia
The film’s formal elements are entirely subordinated to the concept of physical and moral decay. The production design and lighting create a world devoid of natural light. The interiors are damp, oppressive, illuminated only by cold neon and emergency bulbs. The costumes materialize absolute deprivation—characters wear rags that serve as visual proof of civilizational regression.

This visual entropy can be read as a metaphorical model of spatial degradation and an extension of the society's psychological state. The camera frequently navigates through tight, overcrowded corridors, evoking the sense that the characters are not human beings with free will, but rather insects trapped in a collapsing hive. The Dome is not a shelter; it is a mass tomb into which freezing water slowly seeps.

### The Bureaucracy of the Apocalypse
The most terrifying aspect of Szulkin’s vision is not the threat of radiation, but the pervasive apathy. Soft (Jerzy Stuhr), who acts as an official and administrator of social order within the Dome, navigates this world with cynical resignation. Institutions continue to insist on adherence to protocols, regulations, and class distinctions, even as the ceiling of the bunker literally cracks open.

The film thus demystifies the end itself. The apocalypse here is not a grandiose cathartic event, but a slow, bureaucratically managed rotting. O-Bi, O-Ba is a cruel diagnosis of human nature: faced with certain destruction, society does not unite in heroic action, but rather chooses to be lulled by a lie, while institutions mechanically perform their functions until they are swallowed by their own procedures.
