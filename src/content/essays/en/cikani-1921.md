The silent film *Gypsies* (*Cikáni*, 1921), directed by Karel Anton and based on the famous novella by Karel Hynek Mácha, stands as one of the foundational pillars of Czechoslovak cinema. Anton's picture is a fascinating testament to how 19th and early 20th-century Central European Romanticism visualized the so-called "Gypsy myth." The film does not strive for documentary accuracy; rather, it uses the Romani ethnicity as a canvas onto which it projects anxiety, the yearning for freedom, and the inevitability of human destiny.

### 1. Premise and the Archetype of the Outcast
The narrative is driven by betrayal and revenge. A Venetian gondolier, Giacomo (Hugo Svoboda), loses his beloved Angelina when she is seduced away by a wealthy count. Broken and stripped of his original identity and home, Giacomo finds no refuge in mainstream society. Instead, he joins a wandering Romani family and becomes a nomad.

The film meticulously employs the contemporary archetype of the Roma as people standing outside conventional laws and customs—bound not by state borders, but solely by the borders of their own fate. Giacomo travels with them all the way to the Bohemian lands, deep into the forests of the Kokořín region, where the plot descends into a dark tragedy filled with curses, madness, and a fateful reckoning.¹

### 2. Romani Culture as an Elemental Force
*Gypsies* does not present Romani folklore from the inside, but explores it through the fascinated eyes of an outsider. In the film, the Roma figure as guardians of ancient truths—proud wanderers who possess an intrinsic understanding of the forces of nature. Anton’s direction (and the later color tinting of the original print) utilizes these characters to craft a magical, almost oppressive atmosphere. The Romani camp in the wilderness serves as the ultimate antithesis to the cold, rational, and cruel world of the nobility in their castles.²

### 3. The Birth of the Czech Cinematic Epic
Anton directed a picture that sought, through its quality and cinematography, to rival Italian historical frescoes and early German Expressionism. Even though the actors performed with their faces darkened by makeup (which today reads as a problematic convention, but was standard theatrical practice at the time), the film is still celebrated for its emotional power and visual poetry. The director created a myth about people who own the entire world precisely because they possess nothing at all.³
