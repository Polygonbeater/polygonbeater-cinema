> "This morning, a dollar costs 10 billion marks. By this afternoon, it will likely be double." – Berlin Daily, 1923.

A billion marks for a loaf of bread and a dance on the edge of the abyss. This is *Fräulein Raffke* (1923).

Imagine a world where you earn a fortune in the morning, but by evening, it won't even buy you a tram ticket. Out of this absolute madness emerged a unique testament to the Weimar Republic—a film that remains as raw and fascinating today as it was a century ago.

While Germany collapsed under the weight of hyperinflation in 1923, director Richard Eichberg took his camera into the streets to film a biting satire about the people who grew obscenely rich off the nation's misery.

### Why This Forgotten Gem Is So Essential

* **The Birth of an Insult:** Who was "Mr. Raffke"? In 1920s Berlin, the name became a slur. Derived from *raffen* (to snatch or grab), it described the profiteers with billions in their pockets but no taste or morality, buying up estates while the nation starved.
* **Laughter as a Weapon:** A populist comedy that gave Berliners a chance to laugh directly in the faces of black marketers and nouveau-riche social climbers while their currency evaporated. 
* **Truth Without Filters:** Capturing the authentic, gritty atmosphere of Berlin where desperate poverty collided with wild, hedonistic excess.

*Fräulein Raffke* is a haunting window into history, a time when tuxedos and pearls masked the terror of total economic collapse.
