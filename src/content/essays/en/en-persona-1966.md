This study deconstructs Ingmar Bergman's radical work *Persona* (1966, running time 83 minutes) as the ultimate exploration of the instability of human identity. The film analyzes the relationship between silent actress Elisabet and her nurse Alma, whose personalities begin to dangerously blur in isolation. 

Methodologically, the text focuses on Sven Nykvist’s experimental cinematography (extreme close-ups of faces), the breaking of the "fourth wall," and the reflection on the film medium itself as an unreliable mediator of reality. The film demonstrates that beneath the social mask (the persona), there is no solid self, but a deep and terrifying void.

### I. White Light and the Anatomy of the Face

The film's visual identity rests on the brilliant work of cinematographer Sven Nykvist. The use of harsh, almost clinically white light and minimalist sets on the island of Fårö reduces reality to the essentials: the human face. 

Bergman and Nykvist use extreme close-ups that transform the face into a landscape. In the iconic scene where the faces of the two women merge into one, the camera physically manifests a psychological fusion. Here, the film ceases to be a story and becomes a visual psychoanalysis.

### II. The Verticality of Silence vs. the Horizontality of Words

The film's construction is built on a conflict between two principles. Elisabet (Liv Ullmann) chooses absolute silence—a vertical escape from a world of lies and roles. Conversely, Alma (Bibi Andersson) represents horizontality—a constant stream of words, an attempt at social contact and rationalization. 

Throughout the film, these axes break. Alma’s words exhaust themselves against the wall of Elisabet’s silence, leading to the collapse of Alma’s own identity. Silence reveals itself as an aggressive force that consumes everything within reach.

### III. Film as a Broken Mirror

Bergman inserts self-reflexive elements into the narrative—burning film strips, shots of the camera and projector. This constantly reminds the viewer that they are watching an illusion. 

This "exchange of images" mirrors the condition of the characters: just as the film strip tears, the integrity of the human soul shatters. The opening montage of visual fragments (nails in palms, corpses, cartoons) acts as a catalog of the human subconscious, foreshadowing the subsequent deconstruction of both protagonists.

### IV. Conclusion: Return to Zero

The film’s conclusion offers no redemption. Alma departs, while Elisabet remains trapped in her silence. *Persona* ends at the point where all layers of the civilized self have been stripped away. 

What remains is the realization that identity is a fragile construct that can vanish at any time in isolation and under the pressure of another consciousness. Bergman defined modern psychological horror-drama here as a journey into places where names and roles no longer apply.

### Glossary and Bibliography

* **White Light and Anatomy of the Face:** Sven Nykvist's visual technique employing extreme close-ups and harsh, clinical lighting to transform the human face into a landscape for visual psychoanalysis.
* **Verticality of Silence vs. Horizontality of Words:** The thematic tension between absolute silence as an escape from social roles and continuous speech as a futile effort to rationalize reality.
* **Film as a Broken Mirror:** Self-reflexive cinematic devices (such as burning film frames) that break the fourth wall and mirror the shattering of the human psyche.
* **Return to Zero:** The ultimate state of deconstruction where all layers of the social persona are stripped away, revealing identity as a fragile, impermanent construct.
