This study deconstructs Yorgos Lanthimos’s breakthrough film *Dogtooth* (2009, running time 94 minutes) as a radical investigation into the pathology of total control. The film analyzes the lives of three adult siblings kept in absolute isolation by their parents within a family villa, led to believe that the outside world is deathly dangerous. Methodologically, the text focuses on the use of language as a tool of taming (renaming objects), physical and psychological conditioning, and the concept of "erased identity." The film illustrates that the most terrifying cage is not one of steel, but the one a person carries in their own mind due to a perverted upbringing.

### I. The Architecture of Isolation and the Horizontal of the Garden
The film's space is confined to a sterile, sun-drenched villa with a high fence. 

* The director builds a horizontal of an artificial paradise that is, in fact, a sophisticated prison. The parents act as tamers, carefully filtering all external stimuli. 
* The children have no knowledge of television, radio, or the internet; their world ends at the edge of the lawn. This spatial limitation creates unbearable tension, as the viewer perceives the absurdity of the situation while the characters accept it as the only possible reality. The villa becomes a laboratory testing the limits of human submissiveness.

### II. Language as a Tool of Conditioning
The most potent element of taming in this film is the manipulation of language. 

* The parents deliberately teach their children incorrect meanings for words: „sea“ refers to a chair, „highway“ to a strong wind, and „excursion“ to a durable material. 
* By losing the ability to call things by their true names, the characters have also lost the ability to think about their prison or rebel against it. Language here is not a medium of communication but a tamer's tool defining the boundaries of understanding. Without the right words, the concept of freedom does not exist, and thus, neither does the desire for it.

### III. The Myth of the Cat as a Lion
A direct reference to the „beast“ theme occurs when an ordinary cat enters the garden. 

* To keep their children in constant fear and dependence, the parents tell them that the cat is the most dangerous predator in the world, one that devours humans. 
* This scene is the zenith of both absurdity and terror—we see adults barking in horror at a small animal because they have been trained to believe it is a monster. The kitten becomes a lion in their eyes not because of its nature, but because of the tamer's lie. It is chilling evidence of how easily paranoia can be induced in an isolated community.

### IV. Conclusion: The Most Perfectly Tamed Beast
The film's conclusion offers no liberating catharsis. 

* Although one of the daughters manages to ritually knock out her dogtooth (the condition the parents invented for leaving the house) and hide in the trunk of a car, her escape is merely physical. 
* Her mind remains deformed, and her understanding of the world is irrevocably damaged. The film ends in silence and uncertainty. The most perfectly tamed beast does not need bars to remain in captivity. It is a creature that has learned to love its prison and fears the unknown world outside more than its tamer. A true cage does not end at the gate, but deep within a mind that has grown afraid of its own freedom.
