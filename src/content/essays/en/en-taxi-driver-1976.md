This study deconstructs Martin Scorsese's *Taxi Driver* (1976, running time 114 min) as a seminal work of "New Hollywood," exploring the psychological collapse of a Vietnam veteran against the backdrop of a morally devastated New York City. The film analyzes the subjective perception of reality by protagonist Travis Bickle. 

Methodologically, the text focuses on Michael Chapman's neo-noir cinematography, internal monologues inspired by existential literature, and the phenomenological impact of Bernard Herrmann's score. The film examines how isolation mutates into a messianic complex and how violence becomes the only possible form of communication with the surrounding world.

### I. Visual Inferno and the Colors of Night

The visual identity of the film, crafted by cinematographer Michael Chapman, transforms New York into a fever dream. The use of highly saturated colors and blurred neons creates an image of an urban inferno. 

The camera often fixates on details through the taxi's windshield, locking the viewer into Travis's isolated perspective (e.g., the scene where he watches Betsy through the window, creating tension between characters within a single shot). Travis's gaze is a distorted filter of a man who sees the world as an "open sewer" that needs to be flushed.

### II. Travis Bickle: The Existential Anti-Hero

Paul Schrader's screenplay draws from Dostoevsky's *Notes from Underground*. Travis Bickle (Robert De Niro) is the embodiment of total alienation. His insomnia and constant, aimless movement through the city mirror his inner emptiness. 

Attempts to reintegrate into society fail due to his inability to read social codes. Travis subsequently constructs a new identity—that of an armed vigilante, masking his loneliness as a moral mission.

### III. The Ritual of the Gun and Body Transformation

The second half of the film focuses on Travis's physical and mental preparation for violence. The scenes of exercise take on a ritualistic character. 

Travis's mohawk haircut becomes the symbol of a warrior who has fully detached from the civilian world and embraced the role of a purifying avenger. Violence here functions not as a political act, but as psychological catharsis—the only way Travis is capable of "existing" and being seen. The rescue of the minor Iris (Jodie Foster) is more an excuse for his internal need for destruction.

### IV. Conclusion: Ironic Redemption and the Cycle of Madness

The film's ending is deeply ambivalent. Travis, having survived the bloodbath, is hailed by the media as a hero. 

Scorsese employs crushing irony—the society Travis despises eventually integrates him through his own pathology. The final shot in the taxi mirror suggests that Travis's peace is only temporary and the internal tension still exists. *Taxi Driver* remains a chilling diagnosis of a world where the line between psychopathy and heroism is blurred in the neon glow.

### Glossary and Bibliography

* **Neo-Noir Visuals and Urban Inferno:** Michael Chapman's cinematography techniques utilizing saturated neon lights, rain reflections, and windshield framing to reflect Travis's mental state.
* **Existential Anti-Hero:** A archetype drawn from Dostoevskian literary traditions, characterized by severe social disconnection, insomnia, and an absence of meaning.
* **Ritual of the Gun:** The physical transformation and bodily discipline of the protagonist acting as a psychological preparation for cathartic violence.
* **Ironic Redemption:** The societal mechanism that retroactively sanitizes and celebrates an act of pathological destruction as heroism.
