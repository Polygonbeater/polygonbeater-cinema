*Jigoku* (Hell) is not merely a story about guilt, but a film that abandons narrative causality halfway through its runtime and shifts into a mode of an autonomous system. While the first part follows a standard moral drama, upon entering Hell, reality transforms into a closed structure where space, color, and movement no longer serve the narrative, but its execution.

*Jigoku* can be read as more than just a story about guilt. Nobuo Nakagawa transforms a drama into an expressive cinematic vision in which the image itself becomes an active vehicle of punishment. A disruption of the narrative structure occurs, and the environment ceases to be a mere backdrop, actively shaping the viewer's experience.

### The Collapse of Space
In the hellish section, spatial continuity is disrupted. The stable horizon vanishes, locations do not connect naturally, and the viewer loses the sense of navigating a cohesive world. Space ceases to function as a "setting" and becomes a network of situations where the composition of the image dictates suffering rather than character movement.

### The Transformation of Causality
In Hell, the relationship between act and punishment becomes less straightforward. Individual guilt recedes into the background, and Hell begins to function as a mechanism that produces torment regardless of the logic of retribution. Punishments do not act as direct consequences of specific actions, but as part of an environment—as if the world the characters have entered functions as an autonomous system producing suffering.

### Color as a Building Block
Saturated colors—red, green, and yellow—do not act as decoration. The viewer can perceive them as a force that shapes and transforms space. Here, color defines the boundaries of a reality that has lost all reference to our physical world and becomes a key tool in organizing the viewer's focus.

### What We Are Actually Seeing
Nakagawa is not just showing Hell as an afterlife, but as a reorganization of film language. In the Buddhist tradition, Hell is a consequence of karma and a state of suffering; Nakagawa transforms this concept into a highly stylized cinematic experience, where the story recedes into the background and the image becomes the primary vehicle of meaning.

Conclusion
*Jigoku* does not feel like a mere curiosity today. Nakagawa created a film that shocks not only with its imagery of suffering but through its radical transformation of the language of horror. It remains a milestone, showing that true dread can arise not only from narrative or monsters, but from the very organization of the cinematic image.
