## Introduction: Return Over Resurrection

There are horror films that frighten through what they show, and horror films that frighten through what they refuse to bury. Mario Bava’s *La maschera del demonio* belongs to the latter category.

At first glance, it is a simple gothic narrative: the witch Asa Vajda is condemned in the 17th century and executed by burning. Before her death, she curses her persecutors, and 200 years later she returns to reclaim life, body, and power. The key point is not resurrection itself, but the return of what has been repressed.

The opening sequence with a metal, spike-studded mask is one of the iconic images of European horror. Violence here does not conclude the narrative; it initiates it. Each hammer blow opens the possibility of return.

## Psychological Landscape and the Collapse of Rationality

Asa Vajda is not an external monster. She is the product of the system that destroyed her. She embodies the return of the repressed, the trace of violence that has not disappeared, only transformed.

M. Bava expresses this logic through images rather than dialogue. A former cinematographer, he constructs meaning through light, shadow, and spatial composition. The black-and-white cinematography does not depict a realistic world but a dreamlike structure. Fog, crypts, distorted trees, and deep shadows function as projections of a psychological landscape.

While influenced by German Expressionism, the film transforms it: shadow does not conceal the monster but generates it. Rationality gradually collapses under the pressure of a reality that resists explanation. Doctors Kruvajan and Gorobec represent modern scientific thought, yet their epistemological certainty fails.

The film’s modernity lies in the fact that the monster is not opposed to civilization – it is part of it.

## The Ambivalence of Evil with a Familiar Face

This logic is condensed in Barbara Steele’s dual role as Asa and Katia. Two characters share one face, yet represent different modes of existence.

Asa is the return of the repressed; Katia is its normalized form. Their visual similarity destabilizes identity itself. Evil does not arrive from elsewhere – it arrives with a familiar face.

This motif can be read through the Gothic doppelgänger or the Jungian shadow archetype. More important than interpretation is its effect: the collapse of boundaries between innocence and guilt, life and death, beauty and horror.

Asa is simultaneously fascinating and terrifying. Bava does not frame her as a deformed monster, but as an image that demands to be looked at. Horror thus becomes a space of ambivalence in which fear and desire overlap.

## Conclusion: The Impossibility of Death

It is no coincidence that the film encountered censorship restrictions. The issue was not only violence, but the fusion of death, beauty, and desire into a single image.

The film dissolves fundamental cultural oppositions: death is not the opposite of life, beauty is not the opposite of horror, and desire is not the opposite of destruction. Everything coexists simultaneously.

*La maschera del demonio* stands at the threshold of modern horror. It still emerges from the gothic tradition, yet opens a space later developed by Dario Argento, Guillermo del Toro, and Tim Burton.

Horror is no longer about monsters. It is about what returns despite every attempt at forgetting.

The film is not about death. It is about the impossibility of death.

Some images cannot be erased. They can only be covered by a mask.

And wait to reappear.
