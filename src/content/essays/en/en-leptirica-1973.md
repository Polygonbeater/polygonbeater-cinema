The Yugoslav television film Leptirica (The She-Butterfly, 1973), directed by Đorđe Kadijević, represents a formal and thematic pinnacle of Eastern European folk-horror. This adaptation of a classic 19th-century Serbian story prioritizes local folkloric traditions over dominant Western forms of vampire horror, plunging the viewer into the deep layers of Balkan folklore, in which pre-Christian motifs intertwine with Christian folk religiosity. Although produced within the framework of state-controlled television in a modernizing socialist Yugoslavia, its tone can be interpreted as a reminder of a dark, irrational folkloric imagination.

### 1. Return to the Roots: The Vampire as Ethnographic Terror
While Western cinema transformed the figure of the vampire into a decadent, aristocratic seducer, Leptirica returns this myth to its authentic ethnographic roots. The vampire Sava Savanović is not an elegant count, but a brutal, animalistic entity embodying raw rural terror. The film thus functions as a cultural corrective—it rejects Western romanticization and presents the vampire myth in its original, animistic, and deeply unsettling form. This shift from pop culture back to local oral tradition represents a distinct act of cultural autonomy.

### 2. The Village as a Space of Folkloric Experience
The film's plot is set in a 19th-century Serbian village, a community in which local traditions carry greater authority than state, legal, or religious institutions. In times of peril, the community turns to a blend of religious and pagan rituals, incantations, and ancient practices of exhumation. In this isolated space, chthonic forces and folk superstitions become the dominant principle of reality. The film visualizes an environment where fear and death are an organic part of the rural cycle.

### 3. Folklore Against the Certainties of Modernity
Although Yugoslavia in the 1970s offered creators a freer cultural climate than the Soviet Union, official discourse nonetheless continued to emphasize scientific materialism and social development. Leptirica introduces a profound ambivalence into this narrative. The film culminates in a shocking twist and does not conclude with a comforting triumph of rational explanation. Evil is not defeated by logic, but at best temporarily suppressed by a ritual stake. From the perspective of cinematic memory, the film can be interpreted as a reminder that folkloric conceptions of the supernatural persist even in modernizing societies, continuing to shape collective cultural memory.
