This study deconstructs the prestigious independent horror *Birth/Rebirth* (2023, running time 94 minutes) as a radical and highly serious redefinition of the resurrection myth. The film analyzes the clash between two women: Rose, a pathologist developing a serum to revive dead tissue in her apartment, and Celie, a nurse whose daughter suddenly dies and becomes the subject of Rose's experiment. 

Methodologically, the text focuses on the clinical, aseptic cinematography that strips the act of revival of all mysticism, and on the ethical vacuum in which maternal love transforms into a parasitic mechanism. The film portrays resurrection not as a miracle, but as a continuous, bloody, and morally unsustainable maintenance process.

### I. The Clinical Horizontal and Resurrection Without God

The film avoids gothic castles and lightning entirely. Rose's "laboratory" is a sterile modern apartment. Director Laura Moss builds a clinical horizontal here. 

The resurrection of the little girl Lila is presented as a series of biochemical steps: substance extraction, monitoring cell regeneration, and constant replenishment of biological material. This matter-of-factness is far more terrifying than supernatural forces because it suggests that life is merely a complex technical problem. In this world, there is no room for the soul; the body is viewed as a machine that can be rebooted with sufficient ruthlessness.

### II. The Symbiosis of Pathology and Motherhood

The film contrasts two forms of female obsession. Rose represents cold, scientific detachment—resurrection is an intellectual challenge for her. Celie (Judy Reyes) represents instinctive maternal love that refuses to accept loss. 

Their alliance is the film's most unsettling element. To keep the revived child alive, both women must cross the most fundamental ethical boundaries. Maternal love is deconstructed here as a force capable of justifying any atrocity. The rebirth of the child is not an act of mercy, but an act of selfish defiance against the natural order.

### III. Somatic Horror and the Maintenance of Flesh

Unlike zombie films, the resurrected being here is not an aggressive monster. Little Lila is apathetic, fragile, and entirely dependent on a constant supply of "fuel" from living donor tissues (including Rose's own body). 

The film utilizes somatic horror—close-ups of needles, extractions, and biological waste evoke a sense of physical nausea in the viewer. This version of resurrection is not a one-time act (as in the Bible) but a permanent, grueling battle against entropy. The life that was returned is constantly on the brink of decay, creating an unbearable tension.

### IV. Conclusion: The Price of a Miracle and the Infinite Cycle

The film's finale brings no cleansing or moral lesson. The characters fall into a downward spiral from which there is no escape. For the "miracle" of resurrection to continue, ever greater sacrifices must be made. 

*Birth/Rebirth* ends with a vision of total moral collapse in the name of preserving biological life. The Sunday resurrection is depicted here as the ultimate curse. The film thus concludes Easter theme with a chilling message: life without death is not a victory, but an eternal, rotting prison for which we pay with our humanity.

### Glossary and Bibliography

* **Clinical Horizontal:** Visual and narrative framing that reduces existential and spiritual questions to a cold, biochemical level.
* **Aseptic Reanimation:** The process of reviving tissue stripped of religious or mystical dimensions, viewed purely as technical maintenance.
* **Somatic Horror:** A subgenre focused on invasive medical procedures, needles, tissue extractions, and the biological failure of the organism.
* **Ethical Vacuum:** A state where moral boundaries are completely erased to bypass natural death and satisfy maternal instinct.
