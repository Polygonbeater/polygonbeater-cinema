This study examines Stanley Kubrick’s satirical masterpiece *Dr. Strangelove or: How I Learned to Stop Worrying and Love the Bomb* (1964) as the ultimate indictment of human absurdity, bureaucratic incompetence, and technocratic paranoia during the Cold War. The film demonstrates that humanity's downfall will not stem from a grand clash between good and evil, but rather from individual failures and unquestioning reliance on automated systems.

### I. Psychopathology as an Apocalyptic Trigger

Kubrick boils down the Cold War arms race into the microcosm of General Jack D. Ripper's fragile and paranoid ego. Ripper does not launch a preemptive nuclear strike based on rational geopolitical strategy, but rather due to a delusion that Communists are stealing his "precious bodily fluids" through fluoridated water. The entire planet is thus sentenced to destruction because of a personal psychological crisis left untreated.

### II. The Doomsday Machine and Blind Faith in Automation

The concept of the Doomsday Machine represents the pinnacle of technological advancement, with the sole minor flaw being that the Soviets kept its existence secret until a planned Monday announcement. Kubrick brilliantly parodies blind trust in autonomous protocols here. The system executes its logic flawlessly, yet that logic dictates the irrevocable eradication of all life on Earth—much like pushing untested catastrophic code to production without documentation.

### III. The War Room as a Sandbox for the Powerful

The War Room serves as a scathing metaphor for the infantilism of political elites. Instead of cold-blooded diplomacy, the space becomes an arena for egocentric bickering, perfectly encapsulated by the President breaking up a physical scuffle between a US general and the Soviet ambassador. Subsequent communication with the Soviet premier lacks any statesmanship, resembling instead an awkward domestic dispute.

### IV. Conclusion: Dancing on the Ruins of Civilization

The film's ending completely denies any form of catharsis or heroic rescue. The iconic image of a cowboy riding a nuclear warhead like a mechanical bull toward oblivion, accompanied by the nostalgic crooning of *We'll Meet Again*, seals Kubrick's chilling diagnosis. Humanity will not self-destruct in a Shakespearean tragedy, but rather because of administrative blunders, vending machine disputes, and profound systemic absurdity.
