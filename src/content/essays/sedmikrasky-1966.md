The world around us takes itself deadly seriously. State bureaucrats measure living space with rulers, moral authorities dictate proper posture at the table, and architects of human souls plan a neatly lined-up future. Everything is supposed to have its order, its compartment, and its utility. Yet what if the entire polished machine is rotten to the core? What if that admired order is merely a very expensive wrapping for emptiness?

At that moment, nothing remains but to pick up scissors, chalk, a can opener, and a heavy dose of impudence—at least as a metaphor for one of the most radical rebellions Czech cinema has ever witnessed.

## The Hard Labor of Human Puppets

Two girls sit at the swimming pool like wooden dolls with creaking joints. Nothing more than two mechanical figures in a mechanical world. Then comes a line that, in various translations and forms, serves as the film’s conceptual foundation: „When the world is spoiled, we’ll be spoiled, too.“

This is no mere excuse; it is a surgical incision. If society perceives a woman primarily as a disposable decorative object, a cute accessory, and an obedient consumer of official rules, the protagonists refuse to play this farce according to prescribed notes. Going bad becomes a conscious mode of existence and play.

Marie I and Marie II do not cry or lament their fate. Instead, they let themselves be hosted by older men, whom they then escort to the railway station, only to leave them there and let them board their trains alone, while they systematically turn bourgeois etiquette into a pile of crumbs. They exploit the system in the most provocative way possible—by driving its own superficiality to absurd consequences.

## Tamers of Boredom and Scissors to Continuity

What still fascinates us about this frantic dance today is not merely the wildness itself, but the absolute freedom of form. The surrounding world is cut into pieces, tinted purple, green, and yellow, and transformed into a radical visual collage.

* **Editing as a Weapon:** The scissors in the girls' hands do not merely cut props; they become an integral part of the cinematic language. When the Maries begin cutting one another into pieces with scissors, and their bodies immediately dissolve into individual fragments through rapid montage, the film demonstrates that time, space, and the human body itself can be edited. Linear plot and psychological platitudes do not apply to this aesthetic.
* **The End of Object Worship:** The banquet table groaning under delicacies is not a temple of abundance. It is an altar of hypocrisy. And so they run across it, throw food against walls, spray champagne into the ceiling, and use pastries and desserts as ammunition.

> *Does a trampled bed of lettuce bother you? And the fact that humanity, freedom, and thought are dying around you—does that not bother you?*

This authorial rhetorical question, inspired by the film's famous closing dedication, directly spits in the face of those who can weep over a broken plate while remaining silent as human dignity breaks down on a broader scale. This is not a simple defense or apology for destruction—Věra Chytilová places the viewer in an uncomfortable position where they enjoy this destructive game while simultaneously observing its relentless collapse.

## Having the Courage to Be Useless

According to this aesthetic, the meaning of existence is not to unquestioningly serve an ideological plan or conform to someone else's expectations. In a broader philosophical reading, it represents the questioning of all supposedly fixed certainties.

Those who wish to flatten the world into neat little boxes will always reject this untamed explosion. They will scream about waste, anarchy, and disrespect for values. Yet on the ruins of false morality, on the shards of shattered porcelain, and among the scraps of torn paper dresses, a heady sense of freedom is born—one that the film refuses to reduce to a simple idyll, instead allowing it to grow unsettlingly into nihilistic chaos.

It is a lesson that never ages. Whenever you feel the world trying to dictate who you must be and how you should behave, remember two girls wearing floral wreaths. Take mental scissors, cut up the dictates, and eat the biggest cake you can find. Because the most beautiful thing about a spoiled world is realizing that we do not have to accept its rules.
