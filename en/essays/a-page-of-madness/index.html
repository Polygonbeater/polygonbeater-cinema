A Page of Madness (Kurutta Ippêji, 1926)

Movie🎬 A Page of Madness
Director👤 Teinosuke Kinugasa
Year📅 1926
Read⏱️ ~2 min
Educational material

The Lost Shadow of 1926 — an analysis of a radical Japanese cinematic experiment.

The year is 1971. Aging director Teinosuke Kinugasa searches through an old storehouse on his property and, among dusty boxes, discovers a miracle nearly fifty years in the making: the surviving original film material of his own film, long mourned by the cinematic world as forever lost 1. In a shed on his property, the film A Page of Madness (Kurutta Ippêji, 1926) had remained hidden for decades—one of the most radical experiments in the history of cinema.

The story then returns to the mid-1920s, in the aftermath of the devastating Great Kantō earthquake. Kinugasa aligned himself with the Shinkankakuha (New Sensationist) literary movement. Together with future Nobel laureate Yasunari Kawabata, he created a work that fundamentally rejected the conventions of its time 2.

Set within the claustrophobic space of an asylum, A Page of Madness tells the story of a janitor and his institutionalized wife. Yet the film refuses to tell its story through words. It deliberately omits intertitles, which were standard in silent cinema 3. Instead, the viewer is confronted with pure kinetic visual energy. The camera distorts reality through optical effects, filters, and experimental techniques that transform the asylum into a nightmarish space. In-camera superimpositions layer visual noise onto the film strip, while a rhythmic, almost musical editing structure pulls the viewer into the fractured perception of the patients 4.

This is not a film to be simply watched; it is a film to be deciphered visually. For those interested in the craft of cinema, the architecture of light, and the limits of visual storytelling without spoken language, this 1926 work remains essential viewing.
