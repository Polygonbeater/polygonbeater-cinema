The Cremator (1969) – The Banality of Evil as an Aesthetic System

Movie🎬 The Cremator
Director👤 Juraj Herz
Year📅 1969
Read⏱️ ~3 min
Educational material

A radical study of the normalization of evil, where Juraj Herz and Rudolf Hrušínský trace how ordinary human desires for order and purity transform into tools of totalitarian ideology.

Juraj Herz’s The Cremator (1969), an adaptation of Ladislav Fuks’s prose, stands as one of the late, radical statements of the Czechoslovak New Wave. It does not treat the Nazi occupation as a historical spectacle or a moral memento in the traditional sense. Instead, it tracks the subtle, systematic process of an everyday subject’s transformation into an instrument of ideological power. The result is not an allegory of resistance, but a precise study of the normalization of evil.

Ideology as an Aesthetics of Purity

The central character is Karel Kopfrkingl (Rudolf Hrušínský), a crematorium employee for whom purity, order, and the harmonization of the world are foundational existential categories. Nazi ideology does not enter his life as an external shock, but as a logical extension of his existing worldview. The film demonstrates that the key mechanism for accepting power is not fanaticism, but compatibility. Kopfrkingl finds not conflict, but an aesthetic and “hygienic” continuity in the new ideology: cremation is reinterpreted as a modern, civilizational service that removes chaos and suffering. The character’s transformation does not stem from a psychological rupture, but from a gradual reorganization of values, where moral categories are replaced by technical and aesthetic ones.

The Optics of Deformation: The World as a Projection of Consciousness

The film’s visual structure, defined by cinematographer Stanislav Milota, fundamentally exceeds the function of illustrating the plot. Wide-angle lenses and expressive use of perspective create a space that is not neutral, but perceptually unstable. The space is either intrusively close or, conversely, alienated, creating a permanent feeling of reality being deformed. The camera does not act as an objective observer, but as a medium for the main character’s subjective perception. The viewer is confined within Kopfrkingl’s perceptual logic, where the boundary between the rational and the absurd gradually vanishes. This approach is not stylistic indulgence, but a structural principle: the form directly models the content.

Language as an Instrument of Reality-Rewriting

Rudolf Hrušínský creates one of the most precise studies of “civil” evil in European cinema. His performance is based on the systematic neutrality of his delivery—calm diction, politeness, and apparent empathy contrast with the growing brutality of actions that his character rationalizes. The role of language is key here. Kopfrkingl uses speech not just to describe the world, but to reshape it. Moral decisions are gradually replaced by technocratic formulations that eliminate the ethical dimension of action. In this sense, ideology manifests not as conviction, but as a style of speech. Hrušínský’s performance does not work with the external gesture of evil, but with its internalized, administrative form.

Conclusion: A System Without an External Boundary

The Cremator remains an exceptionally accurate portrait of a mechanism in which evil asserts itself not through violence, but through the gradual reorganization of meaning. The film does not depict an extreme exception, but a possibility contained within ordinary social rationality: the ability to transition from moral judgment to technical justification. Herz creates a work where ideology appears not as an external force, but as a structure that can be accepted precisely because it corresponds to the need for order, stability, and intelligibility. In this sense, the film transcends its historical context, becoming a study of the fragility of human judgment under conditions of systemic rationalization.
